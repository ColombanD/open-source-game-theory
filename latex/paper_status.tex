
\section{Experiments/Results}
In this section, we use the framework of \cref{section:Lean,section:Theory} to inspect which strategies dominate, under full and partial transparency. We proceed in two steps. First, in \cref{section:Results-Base}, we look at the zoo under full transparency: all outcomes between programs are proven in Lean, and we analyze which strategies survive in a population. This also serves as a baseline, since by \cref{prop:Theory-Anchor} it is the $t = 1$ case of the partially transparent game. Then, in \cref{section:Results-Transparency}, we vary the transparency $t$ and the caution $\alpha$, and answer the main question of this thesis: how much transparency does Löbian cooperation need? \Cref{section:Results-Discussion} summarizes what we found. In both parts we use the same two analyses, a cooperation rate and an evolutionary analysis, and all results are computed from the certified outcomes.

\subsection{Full Transparency: The Base Zoo}
\label{section:Results-Base}
\input{figures and tables/zoo_nesting}

We start by analyzing the base case, i.e. open-source game theory. We fix an eight-bot zoo \texttt{body} = \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{CupodBot}, \texttt{DBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\} defined in \cref{table:base-bots} and provide the proofs for all outcomes of each pair, either by hand or by an LLM proof agent driving the Lean kernel, built on Anthropic's Claude Opus and Fable model families \citep{anthropic2026fable51,anthropic2026opus48}, which typically took between two and thirty minutes of compute per outcome with one agent.\footnote{From \cref{subsection:Lean-Floors}, we know that some results depend on a hinge (whether a failed search is provable within length $k$). We report the main findings assuming the more conservative result (i.e. assuming $\neg H_k$) and do a comparative study of the other case in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-BaseBots-Hinge}.} \Cref{table:outcome-matrix-body} summarizes the outcomes. 

For this zoo, 20 of the 64 ordered cells are $(C,C)$, 16 are $(D,D)$ and 28 are exploitation cells; 34 of the 64 row actions are $C$. The four outcomes are thus reasonably balanced, which makes the zoo a fair test bed.

\subsubsection{Evolutionary Selection}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}

Our goal with the experiments is to find out which strategies dominate the population, and how robust they are, using techniques from \emph{evolutionary game theory}. For this, we need to fix a concrete payoff matrix $A_{PD}$, which we take from \cref{example:PD} with $T = 3, R = 2, P=0$ and $S = -1$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
       & C & D \\ \hline
     C & (2,2) & (-1,3) \\
     D & (3,-1) & (0,0)
  \end{array}
\]

We run the standard evolutionary stages (\cref{tab:egt-stages}) on the payoff matrix, from static to dynamic.

\input{figures and tables/replicator_body}

\paragraph{Evolutionary selection.} Although there are no evolutionary stable strategies, we have Nash equilibria that fall into three components: a fully cooperative one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, a partially cooperative mixed one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}\}, and an all-defect one on \{\texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{OBot}\} (\cref{tab:statics-body}).
\input{figures and tables/moran_body}

Of 201 interior replicator starts, 88\% converge to the mutually cooperative support \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, and every start ends on a support containing \texttt{DupocBot} (\cref{tab:replicator-body}). In the Moran process at strong selection ($M = 100$, $\beta = 1$), \texttt{DupocBot} alone is stochastically stable, with 85.9\% of the stationary distribution; its share rises monotonically with $M$ and $\beta$, from 14\% to 86\% across the grid, and \{\texttt{DupocBot}\} is the selected set at eight of the nine $(M,\beta)$ points, all but the weakest (\cref{tab:moran-body}). The reason is because \texttt{DupocBot}'s row has no $(C,D)$ cell and its self-cell is $(C,C)$, so every other type earns at most what \texttt{DupocBot} earns against itself, with equality only for its two mutual cooperators.

\paragraph{What this entails.} At the base case, \texttt{DupocBot} is not merely a member of the cooperative equilibrium, but it is the dominant strategy a population converges to from almost every start and returns to in the long run. Indeed, it cooperates with every reciprocator, defects on every exploiter, and can never itself be exploited (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability}) so it is never at a payoff disadvantage against any member of the zoo. In addition, although unconditional defection wins in the prisoner's dilemma base game, it performs very bad in the open-source version: \texttt{DefectBot} holds 4.5\% of the long run at strong selection. Reading source code thus allows for more cooperative equilibria, where \citet{Critch} already showed that cooperation becomes available, but also at the level of selection, where it becomes the outcome.


\subsubsection{Choice of Zoo}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Zoo}
The idea behind the choice of \texttt{body} is that it mixes our two provability protagonists \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot} with simulation-based bots and constant bots, in order to show why provability is more robust than simulation and closed-source game theory. That being said, the results above are intricately dependent on this zoo's composition, since for instance, the overall zoo-cooperation rate changes whenever a bot is added. 

We go beyond this limit by showing that our selection results are not affected by a change in the zoo, and that \texttt{DupocBot} truly is the dominant cooperative strategy. To do this, we run the same selection stages on three other zoos that, combined with \texttt{body}, form four nested zoos. \Cref{tab:zoo-ladder} summarizes the combined results, which all point to the same conclusion: \texttt{DupocBot} is always part of the rationally dominant cooperative solutions, and the only stochastically stable strategy at strong selection.

\subsubsection{Interesting parallel results}
We end the base case analysis with two additional interesting results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unexploitability.} Some bots in our library are provably \emph{unexploitable}: they never cooperate while their opponent defects. In our zoo, these are \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, and \texttt{CIMCIC}. \Cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability} gives the proofs for these bots, and \cref{table:outcome-matrix-body} confirms this empirically (highlighted yellow). Interestingly, \texttt{OBot} also has this property against our zoo, which suggests that a two-probe simulation guard is more robust than a single probe (like \texttt{DBot} or \texttt{TitForTatBot}). However, \texttt{OBot} is not provably unexploitable, and one could write a bot that exploits it.
    
    \item \textbf{\texttt{DIMCID}, and where $S'$ contradicts the source paper.} \texttt{CIMCID} was taken from \citet{Critch}, who also introduces an adgacent bot, called \texttt{DIMCID}: \enquote{defect if my cooperation implies your defection}\citep[\S5.2]{Critch}. In $S'$, we prove that \texttt{DIMCID} is behaviorally equivalent to \texttt{DefectBot}, i.e. that it defects against \emph{every} closed program (see \cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-DIMCID} for the proof of this). This result directly contradicts example 5.3(a) of \citet{Critch}, which gives $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DIMCID}(k), \texttt{CB}) = (C,C)$ instead of $(D,C)$. The very argument \citet{Critch} uses for Theorem 5.5(a) on the self-play cell would also yield $(D,C)$ instead of $(C,C)$, reinforcing our claim. Moreover, this settles their Open Problem 6: $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DUPOC}(k), \texttt{DIMCID}(k))=(D,D)$, which we prove in our library. Open Problem 5 however is still unsettled, as it rests on the hinge, which we leave open.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Partial Transparency}
\label{section:Results-Transparency}
With the base analysis done, showing that Löbian cooperation (\texttt{DupocBot}) is what selection keeps, we turn to the question this thesis is about: how much transparency does that cooperation need? We run the same two analyses as in \cref{subsection:Results-Base-Analysis}, but on the partially transparent game $G_{PD}^{\tau}(s,\alpha)$ of \cref{section:Theory-Transparency}, over a grid of transparencies $t$ and cautions $\alpha$.

\subsection{Setup: Zoos, Aggregators and Signals}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Env}
\paragraph{Zoo.} The default zoo is again \texttt{body}, lifted template by template. Twins matter more here than at full transparency: two programs with identical rows have behavioral distance zero, so a distance-based family can never tell them apart and its transparency has a ceiling below 1. \texttt{body} has no behavioral twins, so every family reaches $t = 1$. Two extensions are used for specific comparisons. \texttt{body+twins} adds \texttt{CIMCIC} and \texttt{PrudentBot}, twins of \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{DefectBot} whose sources are syntactically far from them, so that the behavioral and syntactic families confuse different programs. \texttt{body+twins+natives} adds \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} and \texttt{MinConfidenceBot}, so that the three aggregators over \texttt{DupocBot}'s test can be compared.

\paragraph{Aggregators.} All lifted programs aggregate by the sum of \cref{def:Theory-Tau-Lift}: cooperate iff the cooperation mass reaches $\alpha$. The two natives keep \texttt{DupocBot}'s test, \enquote{does this hypothesis provably cooperate with me?}, and change only the vote. \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{at least one} hypothesis of weight at least $\alpha$ passes the test; \texttt{MinConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{every} hypothesis of weight at least $1-\alpha$ passes it, so that a single credible hypothesis that cannot be shown to cooperate vetoes. With this convention a larger $\alpha$ means more caution for all three. Because the natives are behavioral twins of \texttt{DupocBot}, the aggregator comparison is run on the $\varepsilon$-uniform family, which is identity-based and has no twin ceiling.


\paragraph{Signal families.} We use the following three parametric families, each with one internal knob ($T$ or $\varepsilon$) that is inverted numerically to reach a prescribed transparency $t$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Behavioral.} $s_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, where $d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell) := \bigl|\{\pi_m \in \mathcal{Z} : \pi_j(\pi_m) \neq \pi_\ell(\pi_m)\}\bigr|$ is the Hamming distance between the own-action rows of $\pi_j$ and $\pi_\ell$ in the outcome table.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic.} The same softmax, $s_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, over a normalized tree edit distance $d_{\mathrm{syn}}$ \citep{tree_signal_family} between the programs' abstract syntax trees. Its confusion structure differs from the behavioral one: for example, \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot} are syntactic near-twins (one swapped action leaf) with opposite dispositions.
    \item \textbf{$\varepsilon$-uniform.} $s_{\varepsilon}(\pi_j) = (1-\varepsilon)\,\delta_{\pi_j} + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{unif}_{\mathcal{Z}}$, the structureless control: identity leaks, resemblance plays no role.
\end{itemize}
The two distance-based families are the point-mass family at $T \to 0$ and the uniform family at $T \to \infty$; the $\varepsilon$-uniform family runs from $\delta$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ to $\mathrm{unif}$ at $\varepsilon = 1$. Calibrating all three to the same $t$ is what makes them comparable: at matched $t$ every family carries the same amount of information about the opponent, so any difference between them isolates the effect of \emph{which} programs get confused with which.

\paragraph{Transparency scale and grid.} For each family $s$, we set various values of $T$ and $\varepsilon$ so that their transparency $t(s)$ (\cref{subsection:Theory-Tau-Dials}) hits a target on the grid $t \in \{0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1\}$. The caution grid we evaluate on is $\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.45, 0.62, 0.8, 1\}$. $\alpha = 0$ makes every program an unconditional cooperator and is excluded, and $\alpha = 1$ requires the whole signal to lie on cooperators. The tournament is piecewise constant in $(t,\alpha)$: grid points whose action cells coincide are the same game and share one analysis, so we report per \emph{phase region} rather than per point (36 points give 12 to 18 distinct games per sweep). One difference from the base matrix must be kept in mind: a $\tau$-program always terminates, so a proven non-termination in the base table is artificially set as a plain $D$ in the swept table.

\subsection{Results across Transparency and Caution}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Analysis}
\paragraph{Cooperation rate: the sign of transparency's effect depends on caution.}
\Cref{table:tau-coop-body} gives, for the behavioral family on \texttt{body}, the fraction of ordered pairs that are $(C,C)$, exploitation, and $(D,D)$; \cref{fig:tau-summary-body} (left) plots the $(C,C)$ rate against $t$. At $\alpha = 0.3$ mutual cooperation \emph{rises} as the signal degrades, from 0.37 at $t = 1$ to 0.62 at $t = 0$, while mutual defection falls from 0.23 to 0.08: when limited information is available, trusting programs extend cooperation to programs they would defect against under full information, exploiters included. At $\alpha = 0.8$ it \emph{falls}, from 0.37 to 0.04, with mutual defection rising to 0.64: a cautious program only cooperates if most of the signal's weight lies on hypotheses it would cooperate with, and blur spreads that weight onto hypotheses it would defect against, so its threshold is no longer met.
At $\alpha = 1$ cooperation collapses at the first step below full transparency. Around $\alpha = 0.45$ the two effects cancel down to $t = 0.2$. The syntactic and $\varepsilon$-uniform families show the same three regimes (\cref{apx-subsection:Experiments-TauFamilies}). So the question \enquote{does transparency help cooperation?} has no unconditional answer: it depends on how cautious the programs are. Any statement about transparency has to say which $\alpha$ it holds for, which is why the rest of this section reports results over the whole $(t,\alpha)$ grid rather than along $t$ alone.

\paragraph{Selection: a critical transparency.}
\Cref{table:tau-phase-body} shows the stochastically stable set at $(M,\beta) = (100,1)$ over the grid. On \texttt{body} under the behavioral family, \texttt{DupocBot} is uniquely stochastically stable on all of $t \in [0.4, 1]$ for $\alpha \in [0.3, 0.62]$, and on $t \in [0.6, 1]$ for $\alpha = 0.8$. Below that it collapses, between $t = 0.4$ and $t = 0.2$ for $\alpha \le 0.62$ and one step earlier for $\alpha = 0.8$, latest for moderate caution ($\alpha = 0.45$ still selects \texttt{DupocBot} at $t = 0.2$); at $\alpha = 1$ selection dies immediately below $t = 1$. The right panel of \cref{fig:tau-summary-body} shows what the collapse means for behavior: the cooperation rate of the evolved population stays near its full-transparency value of 0.94 and then drops to nearly zero across the same band, and at the opaque end the surviving types, \texttt{DupocBot} among them, are all defectors. This is the first answer to our question: for cooperation to be what a population actually converges to, it needs roughly 40\% transparency, and a moderately cautious population makes do with 20\%. Full transparency is not required; near-opacity is fatal.

\paragraph{The static and the dynamic collapse coincide.}
The 40\% figure comes from one method, the Moran process, so we check it against a second one that has nothing to do with population dynamics. For each game on the grid we ask a purely static question: does a Nash equilibrium in which both players cooperate still exist? Call the answer the \emph{static ceiling}, 1 if such an equilibrium exists and 0 if not. \Cref{fig:tau-collapse-body} draws this ceiling (dashed) together with the cooperation actually reached by the evolved population (solid), one panel per $\alpha$. In every panel where both lines drop, they drop at the same $t$: the transparency at which the cooperative equilibrium disappears is the transparency at which the population stops cooperating. Two independent methods, one about what rational play allows and one about what selection produces, agree on the threshold. Above it, the population also nearly fills the ceiling, cooperating at 0.94 against a possible 1.

The one exception is $\alpha = 0.3$, where the two lines part: the cooperative equilibrium exists at every $t$, yet the population's cooperation sinks slowly to 0.64 at $t = 0$. At low caution, cooperation remains rationally possible below the threshold but is no longer what selection picks. Note also that the static question had to be about Nash equilibria and not about evolutionarily stable strategies: on 93 of the 94 games in the sweep there is no pure ESS at all (the exception is \texttt{EBot} under the syntactic family at $t = 0.2$, $\alpha = 0.8$), so that notion would have said nothing.

\paragraph{Which programs get confused matters.}
So far the signal was behavioral: a noisy record of how the opponent plays. What if the leak is a corrupted copy of its code instead? By construction, the syntactic family carries exactly as much information as the behavioral one at the same $t$; it only differs in \emph{which} programs it mixes up. \Cref{fig:tau-families-body} shows that this alone changes the threshold. At high caution the syntactic family collapses earlier: for $\alpha = 0.8$, cooperation dies at $t = 0.6$ under source blur but only at $t = 0.4$ under behavior blur. At low caution it collapses at the opaque end where the behavioral family does not: for $\alpha = 0.3$ and $t = 0$, the population still cooperates at a rate of 0.64 under behavior blur but at 0.01 under source blur. Over the whole grid the two families disagree on the selected set at 7 of 36 points.

The cause is one pair of programs. \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot} have almost the same source, one action leaf swapped, and opposite behavior: one cooperates only on proof, the other cooperates by default. A behavioral signal never confuses them; a syntactic signal does, so under source blur part of the weight that should sit on \texttt{DupocBot} sits on \texttt{CupodBot}, whose cooperation \texttt{DupocBot}'s own test cannot certify. The practical reading is that a cautious population needs \emph{more} transparency, 60\% rather than 40\%, when what leaks is code rather than behavior. How much transparency cooperation needs depends on the channel, not only on the amount of information.

\paragraph{Aggregators: pessimist $<$ expectation $<$ optimist.}
On \texttt{body+twins+natives} under the $\varepsilon$-uniform family (\cref{table:tau-aggregators}), the Löbian class \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{CIMCIC}, \texttt{Max}, \texttt{Min}\} holds about 95\% of the long run in an even split for $t \ge 0.6$ at every $\alpha < 1$: the aggregator is irrelevant until blur reaches the thresholds. Below that the three separate. \texttt{MinConfidenceBot}, which any credible hypothesis it cannot certify vetoes, exits first (share 0 by $t = 0.4$ at $\alpha = 0.3$); in a mid-blur band \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} alone takes about 90\% ($t = 0.2$ at $\alpha \in \{0.45, 0.62\}$, already $t = 0.6$ at $\alpha = 0.8$), because the sum player is fooled into cooperating with blurred exploiters while the max player, which needs one hypothesis credible on its own, is not. At $\alpha = 1$ nothing is selected. Once identities blur, ambiguity aversion is what keeps a Löbian cooperator alive.

\section{Discussion}
\label{section:Results-Discussion}

The experiments above give the thesis question a number. On the \texttt{body} zoo, Löbian cooperation needs about 40\% of the information about the opponent's identity, measured as normalized mutual information. Above that, \texttt{DupocBot} is what a population converges to, exactly as at full transparency (\cref{subsection:Results-Base-Analysis}), and the population cooperates close to the rational ceiling; below it, cooperation stops being an equilibrium and stops being selected, at the same transparency. The number moves with three things. Caution $\alpha$ moves it: moderate caution tolerates 20\%, unanimity tolerates nothing. The channel $s$ moves it: under source blur a cautious population needs 60\% rather than 40\%, because a corrupted source confuses \texttt{DupocBot} with its opposite, \texttt{CupodBot}. And the way a program uses the signal moves it: the pessimist needs the most transparency, the expected-value lift less, and the optimist the least.

Two things about these results are worth stressing. First, the selection result of the base analysis is unchanged down to 40\% transparency, so it survives the loss of more than half of the information about the opponent. Critch's framework is stated at full transparency because that is where the proofs are; the population-level conclusion does not need it. Second, the two analyses can point in opposite directions. At low caution, the cooperation rate of the tournament goes \emph{up} as the signal degrades, because trusting programs start cooperating with everyone, exploiters included. But the evolved population cooperates \emph{less}, because cooperating with an exploiter is a losing move and selection weeds it out. Looking at the cooperation rate alone, one would conclude that less transparency helps cooperation; looking at the population alone, one would miss that cooperation was being offered at all. Only together do they tell the right story.

\paragraph{What would change the answer.} A different zoo, first of all. The threshold is a property of this roster and these payoffs, and the twin test of \cref{subsection:Results-Base-Analysis} is a starting point to test this; a strategy that exploits \texttt{DupocBot}'s partners without being detected would lower it, a richer set of cooperators might raise it. Second, signals formed in play rather than swept, and blur that is common knowledge, so that a program can reason about its opponent's uncertainty; both are outside the framework as defined. Third, selection strength: \enquote{selected} means strong selection, robust at six of nine $(M,\beta)$ points, and at weak selection no strategy stands out.
