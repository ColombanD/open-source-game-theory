\subsection{Full Transparency: The Base Zoo}
\label{section:Results-Base}
Using \cref{section:Lean}, we define a zoo of bots (\cref{table:base-bots}), prove the outcome of every pair (\cref{table:outcome-matrix}), and analyze subsets of the zoo: cooperation rates, dominant strategies and Nash equilibria (\cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}). The goal of this analysis is to present a real instance of open-source game theory, and to serve as a baseline for full transparency when we analyze partial transparency in the following sections.
\input{figures and tables/base_bots}
The outcomes were proved either by hand or by an LLM proof agent driving the Lean kernel, built on Anthropic's Claude Opus and Fable model families \citep{anthropic2026fable51,anthropic2026opus48}, which typically took between two and thirty minutes of compute per outcome with one agent; the cells of \cref{table:outcome-matrix} are their restatements in $S'$ (\cref{section:Lean}), one theorem per cell.

\subsubsection{Outcomes}
\label{subsubsection:Results-FullTrans-Outcomes}
% [S'] zoo = body8 (8 bots): 58 theorem cells + 6 hinge cells (3 pairs); twin-free on the engine side (on the hinge side CupodBot's and DupocBot's action rows coincide, their columns do not).
For the analyses we fix the eight-bot zoo \texttt{body} = \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{CupodBot}, \texttt{DBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}: fifty-eight of its sixty-four cells are theorems and the remaining six are the two orientations of three hinge dichotomies, carried at the engine side; none is a non-terminating match; and no two members have the same row of actions, so no strategy is counted twice (\cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Zoo} explains the choice). Payoffs are set as follows:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
       & C & D \\ \hline
     C & (2,2) & (-1,3) \\
     D & (3,-1) & (0,0)
  \end{array}
\]

Before analyzing the dynamics, we want to point out three results on the outcomes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The hinge.} Recall from the previous section that some outcomes depend on a \emph{hinge}: a prover (such as \texttt{DupocBot}) whose guard is a true $\Sigma_1$ sentence, but whose evaluation witness requires a proof of \enquote{no proof of length at most $k$ exists}, whose precise budget is an open question. We thus have a dichotomy, where two possible outcomes emerge depending on which budget we ask. For instance, if the guard is not provable within budget, \texttt{DupocBot} plays $D$ against \texttt{DBot}, but if it is, it plays $C$. Three pairs of the zoo are of this kind (marked $\ddagger$ in \cref{table:outcome-matrix}): \texttt{DupocBot} against the else-branch cooperators \texttt{DBot} and \texttt{EBot}, and \texttt{CupodBot} against \texttt{OBot}. Instead of choosing an arbitrary side, we report our results based on each budget choice, and compare them to assess the effect this hinge has on our findings (\cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}).
    \item \textbf{Unexploitability.} Some bots in our library are provably \emph{unexploitable}: they never cooperate while their opponent defects. In our zoo, these are \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{PrudentBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{MirrorBot} and \texttt{CIMCIC}. \Cref{apx-section:Experiments-BaseBots-Unexploitability} gives the proofs for these bots, and \cref{table:outcome-matrix} confirms this empirically (highlighted yellow). Interestingly, \texttt{OBot} also has this property against our zoo, which suggests that a two-probe simulation guard is more robust than a single probe (like \texttt{DBot} or \texttt{TitForTatBot}). However, \texttt{OBot} is not provably unexploitable, and one could write a bot that exploits it.
    % [new]
    \item \textbf{\texttt{DIMCID}, and where $S'$ contradicts the source paper.} The thesis zoo contained \texttt{DIMCID}, \enquote{defect if my cooperation implies your defection} \citep[\S5.2]{Critch}; it is absent here, by theorem. In $S'$, \texttt{DIMCID} defects against \emph{every} closed program. Write $p$ for \enquote{\texttt{DIMCID} defects} and $q$ for \enquote{the opponent defects}: the bot defects exactly when it proves $\neg p \to q$, that is $p \vee q$, so its behavior is the fixed point of $p \leftrightarrow \Box(p \vee q)$, and in any theory with Löb's theorem that fixed point is $\top$ whatever $q$ is, since $p \to p \vee q$ gives $\Box p \to \Box(p \vee q) \leftrightarrow p$ and Löb gives $p$. A proof of the guard makes the bot defect, which falsifies the guard's antecedent, which makes the guard true; the opponent never enters. Three consequences. First, \texttt{DIMCID} is a payoff-exact twin of \texttt{DefectBot}, and no bot that reads behavior rather than source text can separate the two, which is why it leaves the zoo. Second, $S'$ contradicts the engine's $S$ in thirteen cells of the full matrix, eleven of them \texttt{DIMCID}'s (the other two are \texttt{WaryBot}'s): the engine's rule set cannot derive the self-reading step, so there the guard is unprovable and \texttt{DIMCID} cooperates, against \texttt{CooperateBot} among others. Third, $S'$ contradicts the source paper. Example 5.3(a) of \citet{Critch} gives $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DIMCID}(k), \texttt{CB}) = (C,C)$; in $S'$ it is $(D,C)$, by the very argument \citet{Critch} uses for Theorem 5.5(a) on the self-play cell, a short proof of the outcome yields a proof of the implication, which brings the outcome about, applied without using the consequent. The same theorem settles Open Problem 6 of that paper, $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DUPOC}(k), \texttt{DIMCID}(k))$: it is $(D,D)$, \texttt{DIMCID} defecting for the reason above and \texttt{DupocBot} because it cannot prove a defector's cooperation (the engine had $(C,D)$). Open Problem 5, $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{CUPOD}(k), \texttt{CIMCIC}(k))$, is not settled but named: in $S'$ it is a hinge cell, $(C,D)$ on the engine side and $(C,C)$ on the hinge side, so the paper's question \enquote{which proof search fails, and why} is exactly the bounded-consistency question of item 1.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Cooperation Rate and Evolutionary Selection}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}
We report two statistics that answer different questions. The \emph{cooperation rate} is descriptive: how cooperative the certified table is. It is cheap and interpretable, but it is an unweighted average over whichever zoo we chose, and it changes every time a bot is added. \emph{Evolutionary game theory} is selective: it asks which strategies survive when the population, not the author, does the weighting, which is exactly the corrective for the first statistic's zoo sensitivity. We run the standard stages on the payoff matrix, from static to dynamic, each answering one question (\cref{tab:egt-stages}); the last of them, stochastic stability in the Moran process, is the selection answer the section is after.

% [S'] eight-bot zoo: engine side 20/16/28 of 64, 34 row C; hinge side 24/18/22, 35.
\paragraph{Cooperation rate.} At full transparency 20 of the 64 ordered cells are $(C,C)$, 16 are $(D,D)$ and 28 are exploitation cells; 34 of the 64 row actions are $C$. The four outcomes are thus reasonably balanced, which makes the zoo a fair test bed. On the hinge side the counts are 24, 18 and 22, with 35 row actions $C$: the open question moves six cells, four of them to mutual cooperation (\texttt{DupocBot} stops exploiting \texttt{DBot} and \texttt{EBot}) and two to mutual defection (\texttt{CupodBot}'s detector fires on \texttt{OBot}).

% [S'] 21 extreme NE in 3 components (full-coop {Coop, Dup, TFT}; partial {Coop, EBot, OBot}; all-defect {Def, OBot}); 3 stable faces / 247 supports.
\paragraph{Evolutionary selection.} There is no pure ESS: \texttt{DupocBot} fails the definition only by exact ties with \texttt{CooperateBot} and \texttt{TitForTatBot}, the two bots it mutually cooperates with. The Nash equilibria fall into three components: a fully cooperative one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, a partially cooperative mixed one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}\}, and an all-defect one on \{\texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{OBot}\} (\cref{tab:statics-body}).
\input{figures and tables/moran_body}
% [S'] 88% basin; 85.9% at (100,1); ladder 14.0% -> 85.9%; {Dup} at 8/9 points (all but (10, 0.01)); self-cooperating share 0.92.
Of 201 interior replicator starts, 88\% converge to the mutually cooperative support \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, and every start ends on a support containing \texttt{DupocBot}. In the Moran process at strong selection ($M = 100$, $\beta = 1$), \texttt{DupocBot} alone is stochastically stable, with 85.9\% of the stationary distribution; its share rises monotonically with $M$ and $\beta$, from 14\% to 86\% across the grid, and \{\texttt{DupocBot}\} is the selected set at eight of the nine $(M,\beta)$ points, all but the weakest (\cref{tab:moran-body}). The theorems explain why: \texttt{DupocBot}'s row has no $(C,D)$ cell and its self-cell is $(C,C)$, so every other type earns at most what \texttt{DupocBot} earns against itself, with equality only for its two mutual cooperators. Population share is not cooperation, however: the long run spends 92\% of its time in self-cooperating states, against a static ceiling of 1.

% [new]
\paragraph{Robustness to the hinge.} Every number above takes the three hinge pairs at the engine side. We re-ran the same stages with them at the hinge side; \cref{tab:hinge-body} puts the two branches side by side. The qualitative answer does not move: there is still no pure ESS, the three Nash components are still present (joined by two mixed ones, both carried by \texttt{EBot}, which the hinge side turns into a reciprocator of \texttt{DupocBot}), the dominant replicator support is the same, and at strong selection \texttt{DupocBot} is still the only stochastically stable strategy, at every $M$. What moves is the margin. \texttt{DupocBot} no longer exploits \texttt{DBot} and \texttt{EBot}, so its row loses two units of the temptation payoff; \texttt{TitForTatBot}, which already cooperated with both, closes part of the gap and ties it at two of the nine points. The stationary share at $(100, 1)$ falls from 86\% to 71\%, the cooperative basin from 88\% to 81\%, a small all-defect basin \{\texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{OBot}\} appears with 4.5\% of the starts, and the self-cooperating share of the long run drops from 0.92 to 0.84. One structural change is worth a sentence: on the hinge side \texttt{CupodBot}'s row of actions coincides with \texttt{DupocBot}'s (both cooperate with every reciprocator and defect on \texttt{DefectBot} and \texttt{OBot}), although their columns do not, since \texttt{DupocBot} exploits \texttt{CupodBot}; a behavior-reading signal cannot tell them apart there. The direction is the general one: on the hinge side the Löbian cooperator is \emph{more} cooperative and \emph{less} fit, because the cells it loses are the ones in which it exploited partners it could not read. The strong-selection claim of this section is therefore unconditional, and the share and the robustness count are the engine-side numbers of a bracket whose other end is 71\% and five of nine points.

\paragraph{What this entails.} Three conclusions follow. First, \emph{the Löbian cooperator is what selection keeps}. \texttt{DupocBot} is not merely a member of the cooperative equilibrium; it is the strategy a population converges to from almost every start and returns to in the long run, and the more strongly payoff matters, the more it dominates. The reason is structural: it cooperates with every reciprocator, defects on every exploiter, and can never itself be exploited, so it is never at a payoff disadvantage against any member of the zoo. Second, \emph{unconditional defection does not win, even though it is the dominant strategy of the base game}: \texttt{DefectBot} holds under 5\% of the long run at strong selection, and under 9\% on the hinge side. Reading source code changes the answer of the Prisoner's Dilemma not only at the level of equilibria, where \citet{Critch} already showed that cooperation becomes available, but at the level of selection, where it becomes the outcome. Third, \emph{the other cooperators survive only in \texttt{DupocBot}'s shadow}: \texttt{CooperateBot} and \texttt{TitForTatBot} tie with it in a cooperative population but are exploited elsewhere in the zoo, and the simulation-based probers (\texttt{OBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{DBot}) are safe against some opponents and suckered by others, so none of them is selected on its own. What the population ends up doing is cooperating, spending 92\% of the long run in self-cooperating states, and it does so by becoming \texttt{DupocBot}.

\subsubsection{Choice of Zoo}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Zoo}
The cooperation rate changes whenever a bot is added; does the selection result? Before answering, we say how the zoo was chosen, because the choice is constrained more than it looks. Sixteen bots have cells in $S'$. \texttt{MirrorBot} is excluded because its self-play provably has no outcome, which the evolutionary stages cannot price. \texttt{WaryBot} is excluded because four of its cells (against \texttt{CIMCIC}, \texttt{CupodBot}, \texttt{DIMCID} and \texttt{OBot}) are not stated in $S'$: they are floors of its negated guard that the ledger leaves outside, so no roster containing it is total. That leaves fourteen bots, among which the hinge dichotomies form a graph: a roster with no hinge cell at all exists with nine members, but no such roster contains \texttt{DupocBot}, and the largest hinge-free roster that does has eight members, five of which fall into two behavioral-twin groups. A headline zoo in which the consistency question does not arise therefore does not exist; the question has to be carried, and the previous paragraph is how we carry it.
Within those constraints the zoo was fixed by three rules. Every search-free bot of the library is in, since they are the opponents against which a prover's unexploitability is tested. A prover is in only if its row is not a copy of another member's: that admits \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot}, and excludes \texttt{DIMCID} (a copy of \texttt{DefectBot} by theorem, item 3 of \cref{subsubsection:Results-FullTrans-Outcomes}), \texttt{CIMCIC} (a copy of \texttt{DupocBot}), \texttt{PrudentBot} (a copy of \texttt{DefectBot}), \texttt{JustBot} (of \texttt{DupocBot}) and \texttt{GuardianBot} (of \texttt{TitForTatBot}); the last four coincidences hold on the engine side of the hinge, and on the other branch \texttt{CIMCIC} and \texttt{PrudentBot} acquire rows of their own. Finally, a bot whose guard reads the opponent's source \emph{by name} rather than by behavior is left out of the base zoo: \texttt{CupodTrollBot}, which defects exactly against the literal program \texttt{CupodBot}, is a program but not a strategy in the sense of the previous section, and it enters only as a rung of the ladder below.

\paragraph{A ladder of zoos.} \Cref{tab:zoo-ladder} runs the selection stages on nested zoos. The seven-bot zoo \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}, \texttt{DBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}\} is the six search-free bots plus the one Löbian cooperator, the smallest zoo in which the result of \cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis} can be stated; \texttt{body} adds \texttt{CupodBot}.
