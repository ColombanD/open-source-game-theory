\section{Experiments/Results}
In this section, we use the framework of \cref{section:Lean,section:Theory} to inspect which strategies dominate, under full and partial transparency. We proceed in two steps. First, in \cref{section:Results-Base}, we look at the zoo under full transparency: all outcomes between programs are proven in Lean, and we analyze which strategies survive in a population. This also serves as a baseline, since by \cref{prop:Theory-Anchor} it is the $t = 1$ case of the partially transparent game. Then, in \cref{section:Results-Transparency}, we vary the transparency $t$ and the caution $\alpha$, and answer the main question of this thesis: how much transparency does Löbian cooperation need? \Cref{section:Results-Discussion} summarizes what we found. In both parts we use the same two analyses, a cooperation rate and an evolutionary analysis, and all results are computed from the certified outcomes.

\subsection{Full Transparency: The Base Zoo}
\label{section:Results-Base}
\input{figures and tables/zoo_nesting}

We start by analyzing the base case, i.e. open-source game theory. We fix an eight-bot zoo \texttt{body} = \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{CupodBot}, \texttt{DBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\} defined in \cref{table:base-bots} and provide the proofs for all outcomes of each pair, either by hand or by an LLM proof agent driving the Lean kernel, built on Anthropic's Claude Opus and Fable model families \citep{anthropic2026fable51,anthropic2026opus48}, which typically took between two and thirty minutes of compute per outcome with one agent.\footnote{From \cref{subsection:Lean-Floors}, we know that some results depend on a hinge (whether a failed search is provable within length $k$). We report the main findings assuming the more conservative result (i.e. assuming $\neg H_k$) and do a comparative study of the other case in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-BaseBots-Hinge}.} \Cref{table:outcome-matrix-body} summarizes the outcomes. 

For this zoo, 20 of the 64 ordered cells are $(C,C)$, 16 are $(D,D)$ and 28 are exploitation cells; 34 of the 64 row actions are $C$. The four outcomes are thus reasonably balanced, which makes the zoo a fair test bed.

\subsubsection{Evolutionary Selection}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}

Our goal with the experiments is to find out which strategies dominate the population, and how robust they are, using techniques from \emph{evolutionary game theory}. For this, we need to fix a concrete payoff matrix $A_{PD}$, which we take from \cref{example:PD} with $T = 3, R = 2, P=0$ and $S = -1$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
       & C & D \\ \hline
     C & (2,2) & (-1,3) \\
     D & (3,-1) & (0,0)
  \end{array}
\]

We run the standard evolutionary stages (\cref{tab:egt-stages}) on the payoff matrix, from static to dynamic.

\paragraph{Evolutionary selection.} Although there are no evolutionary stable strategies, we have Nash equilibria that fall into three components: a fully cooperative one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, a partially cooperative mixed one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}\}, and an all-defect one on \{\texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{OBot}\} (\cref{tab:statics-body}).
\input{figures and tables/moran_body}

Of 201 interior replicator starts, 88\% converge to the mutually cooperative support \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, and every start ends on a support containing \texttt{DupocBot} (\cref{tab:replicator-body}). In the Moran process at strong selection ($M = 100$, $\beta = 1$), \texttt{DupocBot} alone is stochastically stable, with 85.9\% of the stationary distribution; its share rises monotonically with $M$ and $\beta$, from 14\% to 86\% across the grid, and \{\texttt{DupocBot}\} is the selected set at eight of the nine $(M,\beta)$ points, all but the weakest (\cref{tab:moran-body}). The reason is because \texttt{DupocBot}'s row has no $(C,D)$ cell and its self-cell is $(C,C)$, so every other type earns at most what \texttt{DupocBot} earns against itself, with equality only for its two mutual cooperators.

\paragraph{What this entails.} At the base case, \texttt{DupocBot} is not merely a member of the cooperative equilibrium, but it is the dominant strategy a population converges to from almost every start and returns to in the long run. Indeed, it cooperates with every reciprocator, defects on every exploiter, and can never itself be exploited (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability}) so it is never at a payoff disadvantage against any member of the zoo. In addition, although unconditional defection wins in the prisoner's dilemma base game, it performs very bad in the open-source version: \texttt{DefectBot} holds 4.5\% of the long run at strong selection. Reading source code thus allows for more cooperative equilibria, where \citet{Critch} already showed that cooperation becomes available, but also at the level of selection, where it becomes the outcome.


\subsubsection{Choice of Zoo}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Zoo}
The idea behind the choice of \texttt{body} is that it mixes our two provability protagonists \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot} with simulation-based bots and constant bots, in order to show why provability is more robust than simulation and closed-source game theory. That being said, the results above are intricately dependent on this zoo's composition, since for instance, the overall zoo-cooperation rate changes whenever a bot is added. 

We go beyond this limit by showing that our selection results are not affected by a change in the zoo, and that \texttt{DupocBot} truly is the dominant cooperative strategy. To do this, we run the same selection stages on three other zoos that, combined with \texttt{body}, form four nested zoos. \Cref{tab:zoo-ladder} summarizes the combined results, which all point to the same conclusion: \texttt{DupocBot} is always part of the rationally dominant cooperative solutions, and the only stochastically stable strategy at strong selection.

\subsubsection{Interesting parallel results}
We end the base case analysis with two additional interesting results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unexploitability.} Some bots in our library are provably \emph{unexploitable}: they never cooperate while their opponent defects. In our zoo, these are \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, and \texttt{CIMCIC}. \Cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability} gives the proofs for these bots, and \cref{table:outcome-matrix-body} confirms this empirically (highlighted yellow). Interestingly, \texttt{OBot} also has this property against our zoo, which suggests that a two-probe simulation guard is more robust than a single probe (like \texttt{DBot} or \texttt{TitForTatBot}). However, \texttt{OBot} is not provably unexploitable, and one could write a bot that exploits it.
    
    \item \textbf{\texttt{DIMCID}, and where $S'$ contradicts the source paper.} \texttt{CIMCID} was taken from \citet{Critch}, who also introduces an adgacent bot, called \texttt{DIMCID}: \enquote{defect if my cooperation implies your defection}\citep[\S5.2]{Critch}. In $S'$, we prove that \texttt{DIMCID} is behaviorally equivalent to \texttt{DefectBot}, i.e. that it defects against \emph{every} closed program (see \cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-DIMCID} for the proof of this). This result directly contradicts example 5.3(a) of \citet{Critch}, which gives $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DIMCID}(k), \texttt{CB}) = (C,C)$ instead of $(D,C)$. The very argument \citet{Critch} uses for Theorem 5.5(a) on the self-play cell would also yield $(D,C)$ instead of $(C,C)$, reinforcing our claim. Moreover, this settles their Open Problem 6: $\mathrm{outcome}(\texttt{DUPOC}(k), \texttt{DIMCID}(k))=(D,D)$, which we prove in our library. Open Problem 5 however is still unsettled, as it rests on the hinge, which we leave open.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Partial Transparency}
\label{section:Results-Transparency}
With the base analysis done, showing that Löbian cooperation (\texttt{DupocBot}) is what selection keeps, we analyze the partial transparency regime and ask how much transparency does that cooperation need? We run the same two analyses as in \cref{section:Results-Base}, but on the partially transparent game $G_{PD}^{\tau}(s,\alpha)$ of \cref{section:Theory-Transparency}, over a grid of transparencies $t$ and cautions $\alpha$. As in the base case, the three hinge pairs are taken at the floor side; the hinge side is re-run in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-Transparency-Hinge}.

\subsubsection{Setup: Zoos, Aggregators and Signals}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Env}

\paragraph{Zoos and Aggregators.} Like in the base case, the headline zoo is the eight-bot \texttt{body}, lifted to the partial transparency regime. We also run a comparative analysis on the same ladder of \cref{tab:zoo-ladder} to see whether the threshold we find is a property of the zoo. Finally, we also run the analysis on \texttt{body+natives}, which adds \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} and \texttt{MinConfidenceBot} to understand the role of aggregators in the dynamics. More precisely, both bots ask the same \texttt{DupocBot} hypothesis \enquote{does this hypothesis provably cooperate with me?}, and change only the vote. \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{at least one} hypothesis of weight at least $\alpha$ passes the test; \texttt{MinConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{every} hypothesis of weight at least $1-\alpha$ passes it, so that a single credible hypothesis that cannot be shown to cooperate vetoes.



\paragraph{Signal families.} We use the following three parametric families, each with one internal knob ($T$ or $\varepsilon$) that is inverted numerically to reach a prescribed transparency $t$ (exact definitions are found in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-PartialTrans-Signals}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Behavioral.} $s_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, where $d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell) := \bigl|\{\pi_m \in \mathcal{Z} : \pi_j(\pi_m) \neq \pi_\ell(\pi_m)\}\bigr|$ is the Hamming distance between the own-action rows of $\pi_j$ and $\pi_\ell$ in the outcome table.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic.} The same softmax, $s_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, over a normalized tree edit distance $d_{\mathrm{syn}}$ \citep{tree_signal_family} between the programs' abstract syntax trees.
    \item \textbf{$\varepsilon$-uniform.} $s_{\varepsilon}(\pi_j) = (1-\varepsilon)\,\delta_{\pi_j} + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{unif}_{\mathcal{Z}}$.
\end{itemize}
Letting $T \to 0$, the two distance-based families are the point-mass family $\delta_\pi$, and letting $T \to \infty$ recovers the uniform family $\mathrm{unif}_{\mathcal{Z}}$; the $\varepsilon$-uniform family runs from $\delta$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ to $\mathrm{unif}$ at $\varepsilon = 1$. We compare each family using the same transparency factor $t$ (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-PartialTransparency-Dials}) so that at matched $t$, every family carries the same amount of information about the opponent, so any difference between them isolates the effect of \emph{which} programs get confused with which. The headline results use the behavioral family, the syntactic family is the channel comparison, and the $\varepsilon$-uniform family is used only where both behavioral and syntactic families cannot separate all bots in a specific zoo (the case of \texttt{body + natives}).

\subsubsection{Results across Transparency and Caution}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Analysis}
\paragraph{Cooperation rate: the sign of transparency's effect depends on caution.}
We first analyze cooperation rates across transparency and caution (\cref{table:tau-coop-body} and \cref{fig:tau-summary-body} (left)). At $\alpha = 0.2$ mutual cooperation \emph{rises} as the signal degrades. At $\alpha = 0.8$ however, the inverse phenomenon is seen: mutual cooperation \emph{falls} as the signal degrades: when limited information is available, trusting programs extend cooperation to programs they would defect against under full information, exploiters included, whereas cautious programs only cooperate with cooperative programs. At $\alpha = 1$ cooperation collapses at the first step below full transparency. At $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6\}$ the two effects cancel exactly down to $t = 0.4$: the tournament does not move. The syntactic family shows the same three regimes up to small fluctuations. With this, we can state the following: overall mutual cooperation of the zoo in the partial transparency regime depends on how cautious the programs are. Any statement about transparency has to say which $\alpha$ it holds for, which is why the rest of this section reports results over the whole $(t,\alpha)$ grid rather than along $t$ alone.

\paragraph{Evolutionary analysis.}
We now repeat the evolutionary analysis of \cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis} on every game of the $(t,\alpha)$ grid and report two statistics, one static and one dynamic, to see how much transparency our löbian cooperator needs. The static one is whether a fully cooperative Nash component still exists. On \texttt{body} it is always the same object, the component spanned by $\{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}$, the three programs whose mutual plays are all $(C,C)$ (\cref{table:tau-ne-fullcoop-body}). It survives down to $t = 0.2$ for $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5\}$, to $t = 0.4$ for $\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.6\}$, to $t = 0.6$ for $\alpha = 0.8$, and only at $t = 1$ for $\alpha = 1$. The dynamic one is the stochastically stable set at $(M,\beta) = (100,1)$ (\cref{table:tau-phase-body}). \texttt{DupocBot} is uniquely selected on all of $t \in [0.4, 1]$ for every $\alpha \in [0.4, 0.8]$ (joined by \texttt{TitForTatBot} at the single point $t = 0.6$, $\alpha = 0.8$), and it is still in the set at $t = 0.2$ for every such $\alpha$; at $\alpha = 0.2$ it is \texttt{DefectBot} that takes the set, from $t = 0.4$ on. However, \texttt{DupocBot}'s selection does not certify cooperation, since it's own self-play cell turns to $(D,D)$ once the signal no longer certifies a cooperator, and \texttt{DupocBot} survives as a defector. The right panel of \cref{fig:tau-summary-body} shows this, through the cooperation rate of the evolved population. It stays at its full-transparency value of 0.92 on every grid point where the fully cooperative component exists, and drops to at most 0.22, and to at most 0.02 for $\alpha \ge 0.4$, on every point where the component is gone (\cref{fig:tau-collapse-body}). Consequently, two methods that examine different aspects, one about what rational play allows and one about what selection produces, locate the same threshold at every caution.

This gives a first answer to our question. Define the critical transparency $t_c(\alpha)$ as the lowest grid transparency at which the population still cooperates. Then $t_c = 0.2$ for $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5\}$, $0.4$ for $\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.6\}$, $0.6$ for $\alpha = 0.8$, and $1$ for $\alpha = 1$. Unlike the tournament rate of \cref{table:tau-coop-body}, whose direction depended on caution, the population's response has one direction for every $\alpha$: cooperation holds, then collapses, and caution only moves the point of collapse. It moves it in both directions: a cautious population needs more transparency because blur spreads the signal's mass onto hypotheses it would defect against, and a very trusting one needs more for the opposite reason, since it cooperates with the blurred exploiters and is taken over by \texttt{DefectBot} (\cref{table:tau-phase-body}). Full transparency is required only of a maximally cautious population; a moderately cautious one cooperates on a fifth of it; and near-opacity is fatal to all.

\paragraph{Which programs get confused matters, but moves the threshold at one caution only.}
So far the signal was behavioral: a noisy record of how the opponent plays. What if the leak is a corrupted copy of its code instead? By construction, the syntactic family carries exactly as much information as the behavioral one at the same $t$; it only differs in \emph{which} programs it mixes up. \Cref{fig:tau-families-body} shows that on this zoo the difference is real but confined to the collapse band. The two families disagree on the stochastically stable set at 5 of the 36 grid points with $\alpha > 0$, all at $t \le 0.6$, and the realized cooperation differs by more than 0.05 at six points; the transparency at which cooperation collapses, and with it the transparency at which the cooperative equilibrium disappears, is the same under both channels at every $\alpha$ but one. The exception is $\alpha = 0.6$ at $t = 0.2$, where source blur keeps both the cooperative equilibrium and a cooperating population (0.94) and behavior blur has lost both (0.01). Wherever the two differ by more than 0.05 above the collapse, source blur is the \emph{more} forgiving channel: at $t = 0.2$ and $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5\}$ the population cooperates at 0.92 under source blur against 0.84 and 0.85 under behavior blur, and at $\alpha = 0.2$ it cooperates at 0.84 and 0.94 against 0.78 and 0.86 for $t \in \{0.4, 0.6\}$. The pair the syntactic family confuses that the behavioral one does not, \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot}, is on this zoo too (one swapped action leaf, opposite dispositions), so the mechanism by which source blur could be the harsher channel is present; on an eight-bot roster its weight is not enough to make it so anywhere on the grid. We read this as a property of the roster, not of the channel: a cautious population on a larger zoo may well need more transparency under source blur than under behavior blur, but we do not observe it here.

\paragraph{Aggregators: pessimist $<$ expectation $<$ optimist.}
On \texttt{body+natives} under the $\varepsilon$-uniform family (\cref{table:tau-aggregators}), the Löbian class \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{Max}, \texttt{Min}\} holds 93.5\% of the long run in an even split for $t \ge 0.6$ at every $\alpha$ (down to $t \ge 0.4$ for $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6\}$): the aggregator is irrelevant until blur reaches the thresholds. \paragraph{The ladder under transparency.}
Is the threshold a property of the roster? \Cref{tab:tau-ladder} repeats the behavioral sweep on the four nested zoos of \cref{tab:zoo-ladder} and reports, per caution, the critical transparency $t_c(\alpha)$: the lowest grid transparency at which the evolved population still cooperates at a rate of at least one half. On the seven-bot zoo, \texttt{body}, and \texttt{body} plus the two trolls, the thresholds agree to within one grid step: 0.4 at $\alpha = 0.2$, 0.2 at $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.5\}$, 0.2 to 0.4 at $\alpha = 0.6$, 0.4 to 0.6 at $\alpha = 0.8$. The trolls change nothing, because a troll that defects against one literal program is, under any blur, a defector the Löbian test already handles. The top rung does move the threshold, downwards: with \texttt{CIMCIC} in the roster the Löbian niche is held by two programs (\texttt{CIM+Dup} is the stochastically stable set at every $t \ge 0.2$ for $\alpha \in [0.4, 0.6]$), the population cooperates at 0.97 to 1.00 down to $t = 0.2$ there, and a cautious population makes do with 20\% transparency instead of 40\% at $\alpha = 0.6$ and with 40\% instead of 60\% at $\alpha = 0.8$. The most trusting population gains nothing: at $\alpha = 0.2$ its threshold is 40\% on every rung, since what ends its cooperation is \texttt{DefectBot}, and no added cooperator dilutes a defector. A second Löbian cooperator is, in effect, extra transparency: part of the signal mass a blurred \texttt{DupocBot} loses to its neighbours now lands on a program that still certifies cooperation. The threshold is therefore not a constant of the framework but of the roster, and it moves in the direction one would expect, lower with more cooperators and unchanged with more exploiters of named targets.

\paragraph{What the section says.}
On the eight-bot zoo, Löbian cooperation needs about 20\% of the information about the opponent's identity, measured as normalized mutual information, for a moderately cautious population, 40\% for a very trusting or a cautious one, and 60\% for a very cautious one; a unanimous one needs all of it. Caution has an optimum: a population that cooperates on a fifth of the signal's mass cooperates with the blurred exploiters too, and is taken over by them at a transparency where a more demanding one still cooperates. Above the threshold \texttt{DupocBot} is what a population converges to, exactly as at full transparency, and it cooperates at 0.92 against a static ceiling of 1; below it, cooperation stops being an equilibrium and stops being selected at the same transparency, under both channels (at one caution, $\alpha = 0.6$, source blur keeps both one grid step longer). The selection result of \cref{section:Results-Base} therefore survives the loss of most of the information about the opponent. Critch's framework is stated at full transparency because that is where the proofs are; the population-level conclusion does not need it. Two further things the grid shows. The two analyses can point in opposite directions: at low caution the tournament's cooperation rate goes \emph{up} as the signal degrades, because trusting programs start cooperating with everyone, exploiters included, while the evolved population cooperates \emph{less}, because cooperating with an exploiter is a losing move that selection weeds out. And once identities blur, how a program aggregates the signal decides whether it survives: the pessimist exits first, the optimist last.
