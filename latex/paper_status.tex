\section{Experiments/Results}
In this section, we use the framework of \cref{section:Lean,section:Theory} to inspect which strategies dominate, under full and partial transparency. We proceed in two steps. First, in \cref{section:Results-Base}, we look at the zoo under full transparency: all outcomes between programs are proven in Lean, and we analyze which strategies survive in a population. This also serves as a baseline, since by \cref{prop:Theory-Anchor} it is the $t = 1$ case of the partially transparent game. Then, in \cref{section:Results-Transparency}, we vary the transparency $t$ and the caution $\alpha$, and answer the main question of this paper: how much transparency does Löbian cooperation need? A closing paragraph summarizes what we found. In both parts we use the same two analyses, a cooperation rate and an evolutionary analysis, and all results are computed from the certified outcomes.

\subsection{Full Transparency: The Base Zoo}
\label{section:Results-Base}
\input{figures and tables/zoo_nesting}

We start by analyzing the base case, i.e. open-source game theory. We fix an eight-bot zoo \texttt{body} = \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{CupodBot}, \texttt{DBot}, \texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\} defined in \cref{fig:zoo-nesting}\footnote{Critch's own \texttt{DIMCID} is absent: in $S'$ it defects against every closed program, so it is behaviorally \texttt{DefectBot} (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-DIMCID}, which also records where this contradicts \citet{Critch}).} and provide the proofs for all outcomes of each pair, either by hand or by an LLM proof agent driving the Lean kernel, built on Anthropic's Claude Opus and Fable model families \citep{anthropic2026fable51,anthropic2026opus48}, which typically took between two and thirty minutes of compute per outcome with one agent.\footnote{From \cref{subsection:Lean-Floors}, we know that some results depend on a hinge (whether a failed search is provable within length $k$). We report the main findings assuming the more conservative result (i.e. assuming $\neg H_k$) and do a comparative study of the other case in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-BaseBots-Hinge}.} \Cref{table:outcome-matrix-body} summarizes the outcomes.\footnote{\texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{DefectBot} are provably \emph{unexploitable}: they never cooperate while the opponent defects (highlighted yellow; proofs, and the \texttt{CIMCIC} and \texttt{OBot} cases, in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability}).}

For this zoo, 20 of the 64 ordered cells are $(C,C)$, 16 are $(D,D)$ and 28 are exploitation cells; 34 of the 64 row actions are $C$. The four outcomes are thus reasonably balanced, which makes the zoo a fair test bed.

\subsubsection{Evolutionary Selection}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis}

Our goal with the experiments is to find out which strategies dominate the population, and how robust they are, using techniques from \emph{evolutionary game theory}. For this, we need to fix a concrete payoff matrix $A_{PD}$, which we take from \cref{example:PD} with $T = 3, R = 2, P=0$ and $S = -1$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
       & C & D \\ \hline
     C & (2,2) & (-1,3) \\
     D & (3,-1) & (0,0)
  \end{array}
\]

We run the standard evolutionary stages (\cref{tab:egt-stages}) on the payoff matrix, from static to dynamic.

\paragraph{Evolutionary selection.} Although there are no evolutionary stable strategies, we have Nash equilibria that fall into three components: a fully cooperative one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, a partially cooperative mixed one on \{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{EBot}, \texttt{OBot}\}, and an all-defect one on \{\texttt{DefectBot}, \texttt{OBot}\} (\cref{tab:statics-body}).
\input{figures and tables/moran_body}

Of 201 interior replicator starts, 88\% converge to the mutually cooperative support \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}, and every start ends on a support containing \texttt{DupocBot} (\cref{tab:replicator-body}). In the Moran process at strong selection ($M = 100$, $\beta = 1$), \texttt{DupocBot} alone is stochastically stable, with 85.9\% of the stationary distribution; its share rises monotonically with $M$ and $\beta$, from 14\% to 86\% across the grid, and \{\texttt{DupocBot}\} is the selected set at eight of the nine $(M,\beta)$ points, all but the weakest (\cref{tab:moran-body}). The reason is because \texttt{DupocBot}'s row has no $(C,D)$ cell and its self-cell is $(C,C)$, so every other type earns at most what \texttt{DupocBot} earns against itself, with equality only for its two mutual cooperators.

\paragraph{What this entails.} At the base case, \texttt{DupocBot} is not merely a member of the cooperative equilibrium, but it is the dominant strategy a population converges to from almost every start and returns to in the long run. Indeed, it cooperates with every reciprocator, defects on every exploiter, and can never itself be exploited (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-Proofs-Unexploitability}) so it is never at a payoff disadvantage against any member of the zoo. In addition, although unconditional defection wins in the prisoner's dilemma base game, it performs very bad in the open-source version: \texttt{DefectBot} holds 4.5\% of the long run at strong selection. Reading source code thus allows for more cooperative equilibria, where \citet{Critch} already showed that cooperation becomes available, but also at the level of selection, where it becomes the outcome.


\subsubsection{Choice of Zoo}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Base-Zoo}
The idea behind the choice of \texttt{body} is that it mixes our two provability protagonists \texttt{DupocBot} and \texttt{CupodBot} with simulation-based bots and constant bots, in order to show why provability is more robust than simulation and closed-source game theory. That being said, the results above are intricately dependent on this zoo's composition, since for instance, the overall zoo-cooperation rate changes whenever a bot is added. 

We go beyond this limit by showing that our selection results are not affected by a change in the zoo, and that \texttt{DupocBot} truly is the dominant cooperative strategy. To do this, we run the same selection stages on three other zoos that, combined with \texttt{body}, form four nested zoos. \Cref{tab:zoo-ladder} summarizes the combined results, which all point to the same conclusion: \texttt{DupocBot} is always part of the rationally dominant cooperative solutions, and the only stochastically stable strategy at strong selection.

\subsection{Partial Transparency}
\label{section:Results-Transparency}
With the base analysis done, showing that Löbian cooperation (\texttt{DupocBot}) is what selection keeps, we analyze the partial transparency regime and ask how much transparency does that cooperation need? We run the same two analyses as in \cref{section:Results-Base}, but on the partially transparent game $G_{PD}^{\tau}(s,\alpha)$ of \cref{section:Theory-Transparency}, over a grid of transparencies $t$ and cautions $\alpha$. As in the base case, the three hinge pairs are taken at the floor side; the hinge side is re-run in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-Transparency-Hinge}.

\subsubsection{Setup: Zoos, Aggregators and Signals}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Env}

\paragraph{Zoos and Aggregators.} Like in the base case, the headline zoo is the eight-bot \texttt{body}, lifted to the partial transparency regime. We also run a comparative analysis on the same ladder of \cref{tab:zoo-ladder} to see whether the threshold we find is a property of the zoo. Finally, we also run the analysis on \texttt{body+natives}, which adds \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} and \texttt{MinConfidenceBot} to understand the role of aggregators in the dynamics. More precisely, both bots ask the same \texttt{DupocBot} hypothesis \enquote{does this hypothesis provably cooperate with me?}, and change only the vote. \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{at least one} hypothesis of weight at least $\alpha$ passes the test; \texttt{MinConfidenceBot} cooperates iff \emph{every} hypothesis of weight at least $1-\alpha$ passes it, so that a single credible hypothesis that cannot be shown to cooperate vetoes.



\paragraph{Signal families.} We use the following three parametric families, each with one internal knob ($T$ or $\varepsilon$) that is inverted numerically to reach a prescribed transparency $t$ (exact definitions are found in \cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-PartialTrans-Signals}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Behavioral.} $s_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, where $d_{\mathrm{beh}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell) := \bigl|\{\pi_m \in \mathcal{Z} : \pi_j(\pi_m) \neq \pi_\ell(\pi_m)\}\bigr|$ is the Hamming distance between the own-action rows of $\pi_j$ and $\pi_\ell$ in the outcome table.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic.} The same softmax, $s_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j)(\pi_\ell) \propto \exp\bigl(-d_{\mathrm{syn}}(\pi_j,\pi_\ell)/T\bigr)$, over a normalized tree edit distance $d_{\mathrm{syn}}$ \citep{tree_signal_family} between the programs' abstract syntax trees.
    \item \textbf{$\varepsilon$-uniform.} $s_{\varepsilon}(\pi_j) = (1-\varepsilon)\,\delta_{\pi_j} + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{unif}_{\mathcal{Z}}$.
\end{itemize}
Letting $T \to 0$, the two distance-based families are the point-mass family $\delta_\pi$, and letting $T \to \infty$ recovers the uniform family $\mathrm{unif}_{\mathcal{Z}}$; the $\varepsilon$-uniform family runs from $\delta$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ to $\mathrm{unif}$ at $\varepsilon = 1$. We compare each family using the same transparency factor $t$ (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Theory-PartialTransparency-Dials}) so that at matched $t$, every family carries the same amount of information about the opponent, so any difference between them isolates the effect of \emph{which} programs get confused with which. The headline results use the behavioral family, the syntactic family is the channel comparison, and the $\varepsilon$-uniform family is used only where both behavioral and syntactic families cannot separate all bots in a specific zoo (the case of \texttt{body + natives}).

\subsubsection{Results across Transparency and Caution}
\label{subsection:Results-Transparency-Analysis}
\paragraph{A critical transparency.}
We repeat the evolutionary analysis of \cref{subsubsection:Results-Base-Analysis} on every game of the $(t,\alpha)$ grid and report two statistics, one static and one dynamic, to see how much transparency our Löbian cooperator needs. The static one is whether a fully cooperative Nash component still exists (\cref{table:tau-ne-fullcoop-body}). On \texttt{body} it is always the same object, the component spanned by $\{\texttt{CooperateBot}, \texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{TitForTatBot}\}$, the three programs whose mutual plays are all $(C,C)$, and it survives further below full transparency the closer the caution is to one half. The dynamic one is the stochastically stable set at $(M,\beta) = (100,1)$ (\cref{table:tau-phase-body}). \texttt{DupocBot} stays uniquely selected on most of the grid, but its selection does not certify cooperation: its own self-play cell turns to $(D,D)$ once the signal no longer certifies a cooperator, and it survives as a defector. The right panel of \cref{fig:tau-summary-body} therefore shows the quantity that matters, the cooperation rate of the evolved population. It stays at its full-transparency value of 0.92 on every grid point where the fully cooperative component exists, and drops to nearly zero on every point where the component is gone (\cref{fig:tau-collapse-body}). Consequently, two methods that examine different aspects, one about what rational play allows and one about what selection produces, locate the same threshold at every caution. The static question had to be about Nash equilibria and not about evolutionarily stable strategies, since no game on the grid has a pure ESS.

This gives the answer to our question. Define the critical transparency $t_c(\alpha)$ as the lowest grid transparency at which the population still cooperates, at a rate of at least one half (\cref{tab:tau-ladder}, row \texttt{body}). A moderately cautious population needs about a fifth of the information about its opponent; a very trusting or a cautious one needs twice that; a very cautious one needs more than half; and only a unanimous one needs all of it. The threshold does not depend on \emph{what} leaks: the syntactic family carries the same information at the same $t$ but confuses different programs, and it gives the same $t_c$ at every caution but one, $\alpha = 0.6$, where source blur keeps the cooperative equilibrium and the cooperating population one grid step longer (\cref{apx-subsubsection:Experiments-Transparency-Channels}). Full transparency is required only of a unanimous population, and near-opacity is fatal to all; in between, the answer depends on caution, and not monotonically.

\paragraph{Caution has an optimum.}
The threshold is lowest at intermediate caution and rises on both sides, for two different reasons, and both are visible in the tournament before any dynamics (\cref{fig:tau-summary-body} left). At high caution, mutual cooperation in the tournament \emph{falls} as the signal degrades: a cautious program only cooperates if most of the signal's weight lies on hypotheses it would cooperate with, and blur spreads that weight onto hypotheses it would defect against, so its threshold is no longer met; at $\alpha = 1$ this happens at the first step below full transparency. At low caution, mutual cooperation \emph{rises} instead: when limited information is available, a trusting program extends cooperation to programs it would defect against under full information, exploiters included. That is a losing move, and selection weeds it out: at $\alpha = 0.2$, \texttt{DefectBot} enters the stochastically stable set and then holds it alone, so the evolved population cooperates \emph{less} exactly where the tournament cooperates more (compare the two panels of \cref{fig:tau-summary-body}). In between, the two effects cancel and the tournament does not move until the signal is nearly opaque. So the two analyses can point in opposite directions, and any statement about transparency has to say which $\alpha$ it holds for; the one statement that holds for every $\alpha$ is that the population's cooperation holds and then collapses, with caution moving the point of collapse in both directions. The syntactic family shows the same three regimes up to small fluctuations.

\paragraph{The ladder under transparency.}
Is the threshold a property of the roster? \Cref{tab:tau-ladder} repeats the behavioral sweep on the four nested zoos of \cref{tab:zoo-ladder} and reports $t_c(\alpha)$ for each. On the seven-bot zoo, \texttt{body}, and \texttt{body} plus the two trolls, the thresholds agree to within one grid step at every caution. The trolls change nothing, because a troll that defects against one literal program is, under any blur, a defector the Löbian test already handles. The top rung does move the threshold, downwards: with \texttt{CIMCIC} in the roster the Löbian niche is held by two programs, which share the stochastically stable set wherever the population cooperates, and a cautious population makes do with one grid step less transparency. A second Löbian cooperator is, in effect, extra transparency: part of the signal mass a blurred \texttt{DupocBot} loses to its neighbours now lands on a program that still certifies cooperation. The most trusting population gains nothing from it, since what ends its cooperation is \texttt{DefectBot}, and no added cooperator dilutes a defector. The threshold is therefore not a constant of the framework but of the roster, and it moves in the direction one would expect, lower with more cooperators and unchanged with more exploiters of named targets.

\paragraph{What the section says.}
On the eight-bot zoo, Löbian cooperation needs about a fifth of the information about the opponent's identity, measured as normalized mutual information, provided the population is neither too trusting nor too cautious; the threshold rises on both sides of that window, and only a unanimous population needs full transparency. Above the threshold \texttt{DupocBot} is what a population converges to, exactly as at full transparency, and it cooperates at 0.92 against a static ceiling of 1; below it, cooperation stops being an equilibrium and stops being selected at the same transparency, under either channel. The threshold is a property of the roster, and it moves the way one would expect: unchanged by exploiters of named targets, lowered for cautious populations by a second Löbian cooperator, and unchanged for the most trusting one, whose cooperation is ended by \texttt{DefectBot}. The selection result of \cref{section:Results-Base} therefore survives the loss of most of the information about the opponent. Critch's framework is stated at full transparency because that is where the proofs are; the population-level conclusion does not need it.

\subsubsection{A side result: aggregators under blur}
\label{subsubsection:Results-Transparency-Aggregators}
The last experiment is independent of the threshold and concerns how a Löbian program should \emph{vote} once identities blur. On \texttt{body+natives} under the $\varepsilon$-uniform family (\cref{table:tau-aggregators}), the Löbian class \{\texttt{DupocBot}, \texttt{Max}, \texttt{Min}\} holds nearly all of the long run in an even split as long as the signal is clear: the aggregator is irrelevant until blur reaches the thresholds. Below that the three separate in a fixed order. \texttt{MinConfidenceBot}, which any credible hypothesis it cannot certify vetoes, exits first; then, in a mid-blur band, \texttt{MaxConfidenceBot} alone holds the population, because the sum player is fooled into cooperating with blurred exploiters while the max player, which needs one hypothesis credible on its own, is not. The band is a band of \emph{survival}, not of cooperation: at the most cautious settings the sum and max players share the long run again, but as defectors, and the population's cooperation rate is near zero. Once identities blur, ambiguity aversion is what keeps a Löbian cooperator alive.
